CamCASP’s ISA-GRID and our real-space route

Both partitions solve the same stockholder fixed point. They differ in exactly one thing — the space the weight function lives in — and that one difference accounts for the per-site parity residual.

Measured 18 August 2026 · PBE0/aug-cc-pVTZ, GRAC, DFT grid 590/99, ISA grid 100/24/32 · every curve and number below is computed from the two converged solutions

1The part that is identical

It is worth being precise about how much the two routes share, because it is almost everything. Both define atomic densities by allocating the molecular density pointwise in proportion to a set of spherically symmetric weight functions:

ρa(r) = ρ(r) wa(|r − Ra|) Σb wb(|r − Rb|)
wa(r) = ⟨ρa⟩(r)   — the spherical average of ρa about Ra
The Lillestølen–Wheatley stockholder condition. Satisfied together, the two equations make Σa wa the best sum-of-spherical-densities approximation to ρ, and the ρa as nearly spherical as an exact partition can be.

They also share the convergence test — Δa = |1 − da|, where da is the normalised overlap between successive iterates of wa — at the same 10−9 threshold; the same 120-iteration cap; the same exponential tail-fix functional form; the same 1.5× radius multiplier setting where that tail begins; and, for oxygen, the same underlying radius. Our implementation mirrors those protocol constants deliberately.

What differs is one decision, upstream of everything else: where wa is represented, and therefore what “solve for wa” means.

2CamCASP: the fixed point in basis space

In CamCASP’s ISA algorithm A — stock(A) in Misquitta, Stone and Fazeli (2014) — the weight function is an expansion in an atom-centred set of uncontracted s-type Gaussians:

wa(r) = Σk cak gk(r), gk(r) = (2αk/π)3/4 exp(−αk r2)
For this molecule the ISA set2 basis gives oxygen 12 functions and each hydrogen 8, exponents in a geometric series of ratio 2 — oxygen from 256 down to 0.125, hydrogen from 32 down to 0.25.

The stockholder condition then cannot be imposed exactly; it becomes an objective. Each iteration solves for the coefficients that come closest to satisfying it in a squared-overlap norm — the run log states the functional outright as ‖ρa − ρ wa/(Σb wb)‖ — by bounded-variable least squares. The bounds are the interesting part: they apply only to the most diffuse functions (exponents between ALPHA-MIN 0 and ALPHA-MAX 0.5), and their purpose, in the users guide’s words, is to prevent “the coefficients of these functions from becoming negative”, which “has the effect of keeping the ISA spherical atomic expansions positive.”

That mechanism is visible in the converged coefficients. Oxygen’s tight functions carry large alternating signs — +3.534, −2.117, +6.860, −0.921, +3.364, −1.914 — while every bounded diffuse function comes out non-negative (+0.175, +0.058, 0.000). Positivity of wa is an outcome of that construction rather than a property of it. Here the outcome was clean: reconstructing the expansion on a fine grid, wa stays positive out to 12 bohr on both atoms.

Why the name has two parts

Converging the basis-space fixed point gives you shape functions. Using them is a separate choice, and CamCASP offers two: FUNC-EXPANSION, which partitions with the basis expansions themselves, and GRID-BASED, which evaluates the converged shape functions pointwise on a numerical grid. The users guide is explicit about the trade — the former “is fast as it works entirely in basis-space, but it is susceptible to small negative terms”; the latter “is slower, but generally yields better” results.

ISA-GRID is the second one. It is therefore a hybrid: the shape functions are basis-space objects, and the partition built from them is a real-space pointwise ratio. Our route is real-space at both stages. That is the whole difference between the two columns of Figure 1 — and the reason the residual against this oracle is small but not zero.

3Ours: the fixed point in real space

We never introduce a basis for wa. Each weight function is stored as its logarithm sampled on a per-atom radial grid — a Gauss–Legendre rule mapped by r = s x/(1 − x) with an explicit node at the origin, 101 nodes in this protocol — and interpolated between nodes by a monotone piecewise cubic:

wa(r) = exp fa(r), fa = PCHIP over { ri, log wa(ri) }
update:   fa(ri) ← log ⟨ ρ · wa / Σbwb ⟩(ri)
The spherical average is taken by explicit quadrature on each shell — a Gauss–Legendre rule in cos θ crossed with a uniform rule in φ, 24 × 32 = 768 directions per node here — and the promolecule denominator is formed by log-sum-exp so it stays stable when one atom’s weight underflows.

Two consequences follow from the parameterisation alone, before any numerics. First, the update is the stockholder condition: there is no objective to minimise, and no residual between what the condition asks for and what the representation can deliver. Second, wa = exp fa is positive for every real fa, so positivity needs no bounds, no penalty and no repair; the partition Σa Pa = 1 then holds pointwise to machine precision — measured 1.1 × 10−16.

The cost is the mirror image. CamCASP does one small linear solve per atom per iteration; we evaluate a full 768-direction spherical average at each of 101 nodes for every atom, every iteration. That is why the ISA grid, not an ISA basis, is our convergence parameter — and why our route is the slower of the two by a wide margin.

4What the two solutions look like

Reconstructing both converged shape functions on a common grid shows how close they are. Over the region that holds the valence density they are nearly indistinguishable; the interesting behaviour is in the tails, which a log plot of the absolute functions hides.

5The tail fix: same function, different anchor

Both routes replace the tail beyond a radius r1 with a single exponential, w(r) = A e−αr, and both fix one of the two parameters by conserving the charge the untruncated function carried beyond r1 — CamCASP’s FIT-TYPE 3, which the guide describes as “the charge-conservation procedure”. Two parameters and one charge condition leaves the second condition a free choice, and the two routes choose differently.

CamCASP:  α = −d ln w/dr at r1   (match the slope)
Ours:  A = w(r1) e+αr1   (match the value)
Each then solves its remaining parameter from ∫r₁∞ 4πr²w dr = the charge the original function held beyond r1. Both conditions are recovered here to six figures from CamCASP’s own published tail parameters, so this is what the code does, not an inference.

Matching the slope leaves the value free to jump, and it does. Reconstructing CamCASP’s expansion at its own r1 and comparing against its own fitted tail:

Neither choice is wrong — a few per cent discontinuity in w at one radius is not obviously worse than a kink in its slope, and both preserve the charge that sets the populations. But they are different models of the same tail, and this is one of the two places where the difference between the routes is generated. The hydrogen step is large enough to be visible downstream, in Figure 5.

6Where the tail begins — and the hydrogen discrepancy

The other place is the choice of r1 itself. Both routes take it as 1.5 × a tabulated atomic radius. For oxygen the tables agree, and the two onsets land 2 × 10−4 bohr apart. For hydrogen they do not: CamCASP’s implied radius is 0.500 Å, ours 0.350 Å.

This matters because the shape functions are not the observable — the partition is. Since Pa is a ratio, only relative tail behaviour survives, and its direction is what the atomic polarizabilities feel.

A testable consequence

The largest single residual in the whole parity comparison is hydrogen’s out-of-plane polarizability: we publish αyy = 0.6446 against the ISA-GRID oracle’s 0.7609, 15.3% low, while every other static component agrees to within 6.6%. Figure 5 shows our partition assigning hydrogen systematically less out-of-plane density than the oracle’s — the same component, the same direction, the same sign.

Figure 4 supplies a candidate cause with a cheap experiment attached: raise the hydrogen radius from 0.350 Å to CamCASP’s 0.500 Å — equivalently, use a larger R1 multiplier for H — so our hydrogen tail begins at 1.42 bohr instead of 0.99, and re-measure. It is a one-line change to a versioned radius table and it predicts a specific direction of movement. It has not been tried.

8What the difference is worth

Against the matching ISA-GRID oracle, with the wavefunction, response basis and fit points held identical, the per-site dipole polarizabilities agree to 0.153 worst case — hydrogen's αyy, above — and per-pair C₆ to 0.099. Against the originally reviewed C-DF oracle the same numbers are wrong by up to a factor of 113. Choosing the matching partition is therefore worth roughly two orders of magnitude on the per-site comparison.

9The coefficients themselves

Ratios compress, and they hide which term is large. Here are the per-pair C₆, C₈ and C₁₀ coefficients as computed, in atomic units, for all three models. Each cell has its own zero-based scale, because within one coefficient the O–O pair is 40–65× the H–H pair and a shared axis would flatten the hydrogen panels to nothing.

The molecular totals in that table are the reason a partition error is easy to miss. Summed over all nine ordered pairs, C₆ comes to 44.16 (ours), 45.66 (ISA-GRID) and 45.58 (C-DF) — a 3% spread across models whose individual pair coefficients differ by a factor of 2.6. Any check against a molecular dispersion coefficient is nearly blind to how that coefficient is distributed. Only the per-pair comparison is diagnostic, which is why the parity test in this pipeline gates per pair.

10What this does to a force field

Distributed polarizabilities and per-pair dispersion coefficients are not just diagnostics — they are force-field parameters. Induction models consume the atomic α directly; dispersion models consume the C₆/C₈/C₁₀ series. Everything below follows from the numbers already presented, and is labelled as an expectation rather than a measurement: none of it has been tested against a fitted force field.

Induction: a quarter of the molecular polarizability changes hands

For a polarizable force field this is the parameter that matters most, and hydrogen is the worst place for it to change. In a hydrogen-bonded contact the hydrogen is the contact atom, so the induced dipole that does the work sits on the site whose polarizability just changed by a third. Three consequences follow.

Induction energy is redistributed toward the oxygen. The molecular response is nearly the same, so a model fitted to the total molecular α — or to a monomer dipole in a uniform field — will look equally good either way. What changes is where the induced dipole sits, and therefore the field it produces at a neighbour and the angular dependence of the induction energy at short range. Fitting to molecular observables cannot detect this; fitting to dimer interaction energies at multiple orientations can.

Damping must be re-fitted, not carried over. Thole-style damping widths scale as (αiαj)1/6, so the change in α propagates weakly but not negligibly — and, again, worst on hydrogen:

The H–H damping length is 12–14% shorter under either ISA partition than under C-DF, while the O–O length is 5–6% longer. A damping parameterisation transferred between the two partitions is therefore mis-scaled in opposite directions on the two contact types. Since damping is what keeps a point-dipole model out of the polarization catastrophe, that is not a cosmetic difference — although the direction here is the safe one: smaller α(H) and a shorter H–H damping length both make the catastrophe less likely, not more.

The most consequential difference for a force field

Under both ISA partitions, hydrogen's most polarizable direction lies along the O–H bond — 0.76° off it for ours, 0.16° for CamCASP's ISA-GRID — with an anisotropy of 2.8–3.1 between the stiff and soft axes. Under C-DF the stiff axis lies 51.6° off the bond, essentially along the molecular C₂ axis, with an anisotropy of only 1.28.

Almost every anisotropic polarizability force field assumes atomic anisotropy is aligned with the local covalent frame — bond-axial. That assumption is compatible with the ISA partitions and incompatible with the C-DF one. A bond-frame model can represent the ISA hydrogen tensor with a single axial parameter and almost no error; asked to fit the C-DF hydrogen it would have to place its stiff axis 52° away from the only special direction the site has. The arithmetic is in the table under Figure 8 — and the two ISA routes, derived independently, agree on this to 0.6°.

Note the corollary runs the other way for oxygen: ISA's oxygen is nearly isotropic (anisotropy 1.06–1.07) where C-DF's is 1.26, so an isotropic-α treatment of oxygen is the better approximation under ISA and the worse one under C-DF.

Dispersion: the total survives, the orientation dependence does not

A pairwise dispersion model sums −Cnab/Rabn over atom pairs. Since the partition redistributes the coefficients between pair types while roughly preserving the molecular total, its effect on the energy depends entirely on which atoms are in contact. So the same three coefficient sets that agree to 3% on the molecular C₆ differ by a factor of 2.9 on the H···H contact energy and by 1.45 on the O···O one, in opposite directions. A pairwise-additive dispersion model refitted from ISA-partitioned coefficients keeps roughly the same isotropic well depth and changes its orientation dependence: relatively less attraction where hydrogens touch, relatively more where the oxygens do. For water — where the hydrogen bond is precisely an O···H contact — that shifts dispersion attraction out of the hydrogen bond and into the non-bonded O···O approach.

Induction and dispersion cannot be retuned independently. The dispersion coefficients here are Casimir–Polder integrals over the same frequency-dependent distributed polarizabilities that feed the induction model. A partition change moves both together, in a correlated way — so a force field that compensates a partition-induced induction error by adjusting its dispersion parameters is fitting one error with another, and will not transfer.

And a caution about our own numbers

Figure 7's rank trend has a direct force-field consequence, and it is not a small one. At contact the higher-order terms are not corrections: at the O···H hydrogen-bond distance the undamped C₆ term is only 32% of the C₆+C₈+C₁₀ sum, with C₁₀ the largest single contribution. The undamped series is in fact already divergent there, which is exactly why every real force field damps it — but the ordering of magnitudes is the point. Our C₈ and C₁₀ run 21% and 28% below the matching oracle, so a dispersion model built on this pipeline's current output would underbind at short range by appreciably more than the 3% C₆ agreement suggests. The C₆ agreement is not evidence that the short-range dispersion is right. That gap is the constrained-fit residual of Figure 7, and it should be closed before these coefficients are used to parameterise anything.

11Summary

The two routes are the same physics solved in two different function spaces. CamCASP represents each spherical weight function in a small s-Gaussian basis and recovers it by bounded least squares — fast, needing explicit machinery to stay positive, and leaving a documented ~10−3 e charge violation. We represent it as a log-sampled radial function and set it to the spherical average directly — positive and charge-exact by construction, and considerably slower. ISA-GRID then names the choice to use CamCASP’s basis-space shape functions through a real-space pointwise ratio, which is why the two agree closely at all, and why they still do not agree exactly.

Of the residual that remains, the traceable part is hydrogen’s: two different radius conventions put the tail-fix onset 30% apart, our hydrogen weight falls off too fast out of plane, and hydrogen’s out-of-plane polarizability comes out 15% low. The rest of the parity gap — the growth of the dispersion deficit with rank — is not a partition effect at all, and has to be chased in the constrained L3 refinement instead.

None of this is bookkeeping. The partition decides where a force field's induced dipoles sit and how its dispersion attraction is distributed between contact types, and it does so while leaving the molecular observables — total polarizability, molecular C₆ — almost unchanged, so those observables cannot be used to choose between the two. The sharpest discriminator found here is the direction of hydrogen's polarizability: bond-axial under both ISA partitions, 52° off the bond under C-DF. A bond-frame anisotropic model, which is what most polarizable force fields assume, is compatible with one and not the other.
